Foundations

Why project management exists

An AI system can draft a plan in seconds. It cannot, on its own, guarantee the plan is true — that the dates are achievable, the dependencies are real, the budget adds up, and the risks that will actually sink the project are named rather than glossed over. Project management is the discipline that converts an ambition ("ship ClaimSight AI by July") into a structure that can be checked, sequenced, resourced, and defended.

As an AI Solution Architect, you are rarely the one holding the Gantt chart. You are the one an AI-generated plan gets handed to for a sanity check before it goes to a sponsor, a steering committee, or a board. Your job is not to become a project manager — it is to know the ~20% of PM technique that lets you ask the right five questions and catch the plan that looks complete but isn't.

Why this matters for an architect A technically elegant AI architecture delivered three months late and 40% over budget is a failed project, regardless of model accuracy. The discipline that protects against that outcome is project management — and you are the last human checkpoint before an AI-generated plan becomes a commitment.
Foundations

The 20% to know

Nine techniques cover almost every plan you will ever review. Master these and you can evaluate a plan in any industry.

1. The scope / schedule / cost triangle

Every project is constrained by three variables that trade against each other: what gets built (scope), by when (schedule), and for how much (cost). You cannot fix all three and still absorb a shock — something has to give. A well-formed plan states, explicitly, which side of the triangle is fixed, which is the primary lever, and which absorbs the risk.

The iron triangle — ClaimSight AI
Stated constraint — ClaimSight AI Cost is fixed at $1,240,000 (board-approved, capped). Schedule has moderate flexibility through three phased go/no-go gates. Scope is the primary lever: if the fraud-signal model underperforms, Meridian ships the triage classifier alone rather than slip cost or date. Any AI-generated plan that doesn't name its fixed side should be sent back.

2. Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)

A WBS decomposes the total scope into work packages small enough to estimate and assign. The test of a good WBS: every package has a single owner, a deliverable you could point to, and the percentages of all top-level packages sum to exactly 100% of scope. If they sum to 87% or 114%, either scope is missing or something has been double-counted. See the full ClaimSight AI WBS.

3. Critical path

The critical path is the longest chain of dependent tasks from project start to finish — it determines the earliest possible completion date. Tasks on it have zero float (zero slack); delay any one of them and the whole project slips day for day. Tasks off the critical path have float and can absorb some delay without moving the finish date. A plan that hasn't named its critical path hasn't been scheduled — it's been listed.

Architect's check Ask an AI-generated schedule: "which tasks are on the critical path, and what is the float on everything else?" If it can't answer, the schedule is decorative, not load-bearing.

4. Milestones and gates

A milestone is a zero-duration checkpoint marking a significant event (a deliverable complete, a decision made). A gate is a milestone with teeth: explicit exit criteria that must be met before work downstream is authorized to proceed, usually with a go/no-go decision by the sponsor or steering committee. ClaimSight AI has three gates — see the Milestone Timeline.

5. RAID — Risks, Assumptions, Issues, Dependencies

CategoryDefinition
RiskA future event that might happen and would affect the project if it did. Rated by likelihood × impact.
AssumptionSomething treated as true for planning purposes but not yet verified. If wrong, the plan is wrong.
IssueA risk that has already materialized — it is happening now and needs active management.
DependencySomething the project needs from outside its own control (another team, a vendor, a system) to proceed.

Every RAID row needs exactly one named owner and a date. An unowned risk is not a managed risk. See the full ClaimSight AI RAID Log.

6. RACI

For every significant activity: who is Responsible (does the work), Accountable (owns the outcome — exactly one person), Consulted (gives input before the decision), and Informed (told after). The most common defect in an AI-generated RACI is more than one Accountable per row — that isn't shared ownership, it's no ownership. See the ClaimSight AI RACI Matrix.

7. Agile vs. waterfall vs. hybrid

ModelHow it sequences workBest suited for
WaterfallLinear phases, each completed before the next begins. Heavy upfront planning.Fixed, well-understood scope; regulatory or contractual sign-offs (e.g., compliance validation gates).
AgileShort, timeboxed iterations (sprints) delivering working increments; scope adapts as learning happens.Model development, UI/UX, anything where requirements sharpen through feedback.
HybridWaterfall "spine" of phases and gates, with agile sprints inside individual phases.Most enterprise AI initiatives — including ClaimSight AI, which runs agile sprints inside a gated program structure.

8. Velocity and burndown

Velocity is the average amount of work (usually story points) a team completes per sprint, measured over recent history — it's how you forecast the next several sprints without guessing. A burndown chart plots remaining work against time within a single sprint or release: the ideal line is a straight diagonal from committed work to zero; the actual line shows real progress. A flattening actual line signals a blocker before it becomes a missed date. See the Sprint Plan for a live example.

9. Status reporting

Status is reported on a cadence (usually weekly or bi-weekly) using RAG — Red / Amber / Green — against the original baseline, not against last week's report (so slow drift doesn't hide as "still Amber"). A good status report states what changed, why, and what decision is needed from the reader. See the ClaimSight AI Status Report.

Good vs. bad plans

The fastest way to evaluate an AI-generated plan is to run it against this checklist. Two or more items in the "bad" column is a plan that needs to go back for rework before it reaches a sponsor.

SignalGood planBad plan
EstimatesRanges with buffer (e.g., "5 weeks ± 1"), buffer placed at the project level, not silently in every taskSingle-point estimates ("5 weeks") with no stated confidence or buffer
Critical pathNamed explicitly; float on every non-critical task is statedA list of tasks with dates but no stated dependency chain
OwnershipEvery deliverable, risk, and RACI row has exactly one accountable ownerOwnership implied by team name ("Data Science will handle it") rather than a named person
Fixed constraintStates plainly which of scope/schedule/cost is fixed and which absorbs changeSilent on the triangle — implies all three are fixed simultaneously
GatesExplicit, checkable exit criteria ("SIU validates ≥95% precision on 500-claim sample")Vague gates ("model is ready," "team is confident")
Risk registerSpecific, dated, owned risks tied to this projectGeneric boilerplate risks copy-pasted from a template ("scope creep," "resourcing")
DependenciesExternal dependencies named with the actual team/vendor and a need-by dateDependencies mentioned narratively with no owner or date
WBS completenessWork packages sum to 100% of scope, independently verifiablePackages don't sum cleanly, or major scope (e.g., compliance review) is missing entirely
Rule of thumb "Hope is not a plan." If a plan's only answer to "what happens if X fails" is confidence rather than a named fallback, it is not yet a plan — it is an intention.

Foundations

The architect's review lens

When an AI system hands you a plan — a charter, a WBS, a schedule, a RAID log — run it through this lens before it goes anywhere near a sponsor.

#Question to ask the planWhere it should be answered
1Which side of scope / schedule / cost is fixed, and is that stated explicitly?Charter
2Does the WBS sum to 100% of scope, with one owner per package?WBS
3Is the critical path named, and does every non-critical task show its float?Timeline / Gantt
4Does every risk, assumption, issue, and dependency have exactly one owner and a date?RAID Log
5Is there exactly one Accountable per activity in the RACI — no more, no fewer?RACI Matrix
6Do estimates carry a stated buffer rather than a bare single number?WBS, Sprint Plan, Timeline
7Are gate exit criteria specific enough that two different reviewers would reach the same pass/fail verdict?Timeline / Gantt
8Does the status report compare to the original baseline, not last period's report?Status Report
9Does the budget show a forecast-to-complete, not just spend-to-date?Budget / Burn
10For every failure mode named, is there a stated retry/fallback/escalation path — not just detection?RAID Log, Status Report
Bottom line You are not there to write the plan. You are there to make sure the plan cannot quietly fail without someone finding out in time to act.

1. Purpose

Meridian Mutual Insurance receives approximately 41,000 First Notice of Loss (FNOL) claims per year across auto, homeowners, and small commercial lines. Manual triage — routing each claim to the correct adjuster tier and flagging potential fraud indicators — currently takes an average of 3.4 business days and relies entirely on adjuster judgment at first contact. ClaimSight AI introduces an AI-assisted triage and fraud-signal layer in front of the existing claims workflow (Guidewire ClaimCenter) to cut initial routing time, standardize fraud-signal detection, and free senior adjusters for complex claims.

2. Sponsor and authority

RoleNameAuthority granted
Executive SponsorDenise Okafor, VP Claims OperationsFinal authority on scope trade-offs, budget release by phase, and go/no-go at each gate.
Steering CommitteeVP Claims Ops (chair), CISO delegate, Chief Actuary delegate, VP Data & AnalyticsReviews gate exit criteria; escalation path for cross-functional conflicts.
AI Solution Architect (PM of record)Assigned at kickoffDay-to-day authority to sequence work, approve within-budget vendor spend under $15,000, and direct the project team.

3. Business case

  • Reduce average FNOL-to-routing time from 3.4 business days to under 4 business hours for the ~70% of claims the model can confidently triage.
  • Surface fraud signals at intake rather than after adjuster review, targeting a 15% lift in Special Investigations Unit (SIU) referral precision.
  • Redeploy an estimated 6,200 adjuster-hours/year currently spent on manual first-pass triage toward complex claims and customer contact.

4. Objectives (SMART)

Triage classifier accuracy≥ 90% agreement with senior-adjuster routing decision on held-out test setGate 2 (Apr 24, 2026)
Fraud signal precision≥ 85% precision on SIU-validated sample (500 historical claims)Gate 2 (Apr 24, 2026)
FNOL routing time< 4 business hours for auto-triaged claimsPilot exit (Jun 12, 2026)

Program costAt or under $1,240,000Rollout complete (Jul 10, 2026)

5. Scope boundaries

In scope

  • FNOL triage classifier for auto, homeowners, and small commercial claim intake.
  • Fraud-signal scoring engine surfaced to adjusters and SIU at intake, shadow-validated before go-live.
  • Integration with Guidewire ClaimCenter and ImageRight for document ingestion.
  • Adjuster-facing UI (triage queue, fraud-signal flag, explainability panel).
  • Pilot at two regional claims centers (Columbus, OH and Tampa, FL) before enterprise rollout.
  • Model risk review and SIU validation as a formal gate.

Out of scope

  • Automated claim adjudication or payment authorization — ClaimSight AI recommends and routes; it never approves a payout.
  • Commercial lines above $250,000 TIV (routed to specialty desk unchanged).
  • Litigation and subrogation workflows.
  • Replacement of the core Guidewire platform.

6. Success criteria

The project is successful if, by Jul 10, 2026: the triage classifier and fraud-signal engine are live across all regional claims centers; the objectives in Section 4 are met or a documented, sponsor-accepted variance exists; and Hypercare exits with no Severity-1 incidents open.

7. Key assumptions and constraints

  • Assumption: Three years of labeled historical FNOL data (≈124,000 claims) is accessible and usable for training under Meridian's existing data governance policy.
  • Assumption: Guidewire ClaimCenter exposes a stable API for real-time claim intake events (confirmed with Platform Engineering at kickoff).
  • Constraint: No production model may auto-close or auto-deny a claim; human-in-the-loop is mandatory per Meridian's AI governance policy.
  • Constraint: SIU must formally validate fraud-signal precision before any fraud flag is adjuster-visible in production.

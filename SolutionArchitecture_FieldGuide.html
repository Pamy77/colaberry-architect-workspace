Case Study Brief: ClaimSight AI

Meridian Mutual Insurance is a regional property & casualty carrier serving roughly 1.2 million policyholders across 14 states, writing personal auto and homeowners lines. Claims intake volume has grown 40% over three years without a matching increase in adjuster headcount. Average time from First Notice of Loss (FNOL) to adjuster assignment is 14 hours; severity triage is inconsistent across regional call centers; and the Special Investigations Unit (SIU) fraud-referral rate sits below industry benchmark, suggesting under-detection. Catastrophe events (regional hurricanes) spike FNOL volume 5–8x within 72 hours and repeatedly overwhelm manual intake.

The initiative: ClaimSight AI is an AI-augmented claims intake, triage, and fraud-signal platform. It ingests FNOL submissions from web, mobile, and call-center channels; extracts structured data from photos, forms, and handwritten police reports; assigns a severity tier and routes each claim to the right queue; and produces a fraud-signal score with explainable reason codes for SIU review — while integrating with Meridian's existing legacy systems rather than replacing them.

Foundations — The 20% You Need

Why Solution Architecture Exists

Software requirements describe what a system should do. Architecture decides how it is structured to keep doing that under load, under attack, under regulatory scrutiny, and under three years of feature changes by engineers who didn't build it. Structural decisions — how services are divided, where data lives, what's synchronous versus asynchronous — are the most expensive kind to reverse. Solution architecture exists to make those decisions deliberately, with trade-offs stated out loud, instead of letting them emerge by accident from whoever wrote the first version of the code.

That matters more, not less, when the first draft of a design is AI-generated. An AI can produce a plausible-looking Solution Architecture Document in seconds. It cannot be accountable for the consequences of getting the load estimate wrong, missing a compliance boundary, or picking a pattern that collapses at year two. That accountability is the architect's job. This guide teaches the minimum vocabulary and review discipline to direct that AI-generated draft toward the right questions, evaluate whether its answers hold up, and approve only what actually survives scrutiny.

Quality Attributes / Non-Functional Requirements

Quality attributes (the "-ilities") are requirements about how well the system does what it does, not what it does: performance, availability, scalability, security, maintainability, observability, usability. They are easy to gesture at ("it should be fast and secure") and worthless until each one has a measurable target and a way to verify it. "Fast" is not a requirement. "p95 API latency under 800ms at 1,100 requests/hour" is.

The architect's rule

If a quality attribute can't be measured, it can't be designed for, and it can't be verified at review time. Every NFR in this guide's NFR Specification carries a number and a measurement method.

Architecture Styles

Three styles cover most real decisions. None is universally "better" — each trades coupling, operational complexity, and failure isolation differently.

StyleFits whenCosts
Monolith / Modular MonolithSmall team, low-to-moderate scale, need to move fast without distributed-systems overhead. Module boundaries kept clean inside one deployable.Whole app scales and deploys together; one bad module can take down the process; harder to give one hot path independent scaling.
Service-Oriented / MicroservicesIndependent teams need independent deploy cadence; different components have very different scaling or reliability profiles.Network calls replace function calls; distributed transactions, versioning, and operational overhead (more services to monitor, deploy, secure).
Event-DrivenProducers and consumers must be decoupled in time; bursty/unpredictable load needs buffering; an audit trail of what happened is valuable.Eventual consistency; harder to reason about a single request's end-to-end path; requires investment in schema governance and dead-letter handling.

ClaimSight AI uses event-driven microservices — justified in ADR-001 against the specific load profile above, not chosen by default.

Architecture Decision Records

An ADR is a short, permanent record of one significant decision: the context that forced it, the decision made, the alternatives that were rejected and why, and the consequences accepted. ADRs exist so a decision can be reviewed and revisited with its original reasoning intact — six months later, no one has to guess whether "we chose Postgres" meant "we evaluated four options" or "someone typed the first thing that came to mind."

Six real ADRs for ClaimSight AI are in Deliverable 3. Each states what was rejected — an ADR with no rejected alternatives is a decision no one actually questioned.

Trade-off Analysis

Every non-trivial architecture decision costs something. Trade-off analysis makes that cost explicit and comparable, typically by scoring competing options against weighted criteria (an ATAM-style approach). The output is not "the math decided" — the output is a defensible, written comparison an architect can override with a stated reason. Deliverable 5 does this twice for ClaimSight AI, and in one case the numerically-highest option is explicitly not the one chosen — because a gating requirement mattered more than the weighted blend. That override, written down, is the trade-off analysis working correctly.

Key Patterns

PatternProblem it solves
API GatewaySingle entry point for auth, rate-limiting, and routing across many backend services.
Event SourcingPersist state as an append-only sequence of events instead of overwriting rows — gives you a full audit trail and replayability.
CQRSSplit the read model from the write model when their scaling or shape needs diverge sharply.
SagaCoordinate a multi-step transaction across services without a distributed lock, using compensating actions on failure.
Circuit BreakerStop calling a failing dependency after N failures in a window, so one slow upstream doesn't cascade into a full outage.
Strangler FigMigrate off a legacy system incrementally by routing traffic to new services piece by piece, instead of a big-bang rewrite.
Anti-Corruption LayerAn adapter that translates a legacy system's data model at the boundary, so its quirks don't leak into the new domain model.
BulkheadIsolate resource pools (threads, connections) per dependency so one overloaded dependency can't starve the rest of the system.

ClaimSight AI uses five of these directly — see the Container and Component diagrams in Deliverable 2 and ADR-005's Anti-Corruption Layer around the legacy PAS.

Fitness Functions

A fitness function is an automated, repeatable check that a specific architecture characteristic still holds — run continuously, not just verified once at review time. Examples: a CI check that fails the build if a service adds a dependency on a database it doesn't own; a nightly synthetic-load test that fails if p95 triage latency exceeds 5 seconds; a static-analysis rule that fails if a new service exceeds a dependency-count threshold. Fitness functions turn "we designed it to be scalable" into something that keeps being true after the tenth unrelated feature ships.

Build vs Buy

Buy commodity capability; build your differentiator. The structured version of that instinct asks four questions: (1) Is this capability core to what makes the business competitive, or is it table-stakes infrastructure everyone needs? (2) What is the 3-year total cost of ownership for each path, including the engineering time to build and operate? (3) What's the vendor risk — lock-in, outage history, roadmap dependency? (4) What does each path do to time-to-market? ClaimSight AI applies this directly in ADR-003 (document extraction) and ADR-006 (LLM hosting) — both buy, for the same reason: document intelligence and foundation-model hosting are not what makes Meridian's claims process competitive; getting to market in 9 months with SOC 2-compliant infrastructure is.

Good vs Bad Architecture

Good architecture

  • Every structural choice traces back to a business goal or a measured constraint.
  • Trade-offs are written down, including the costs of the option chosen.
  • Right-sized for current scale plus near-term growth — not built for a scale that may never arrive.
  • Boring, proven technology where nothing about the problem demands otherwise.
  • Fitness functions and tests protect the characteristics that matter.
  • Failure modes are designed, not discovered in production.

Bad architecture

  • Technology chosen for résumé value or hype, not fit.
  • "Best practice" copied without asking whether the context matches.
  • Decisions live only in one person's head — no ADRs, no paper trail.
  • Over-engineered for imagined future scale that displaces the actual 9-month deadline.
  • Tight coupling relabeled as "integration."
  • Security and compliance bolted on after the design is "done."

The Architect's Review Lens

This is the checklist to run against any AI-generated design before approving it — including the eight documents in this guide.

  • Does every quality attribute have a measurable target and a measurement method?
  • Is the architecture style justified against actual load and growth — not assumed by default?
  • Does every significant decision have an ADR, including rejected alternatives?
  • Do trade-off write-ups state costs of the chosen option, not just its wins?
  • Is there evidence against over-engineering — a YAGNI check on anything built for scale that isn't required yet?
  • Are failure modes and degraded paths defined for every external dependency?
  • Is data ownership and consistency boundary unambiguous?
  • Is security and compliance designed in from the start, not appended?
  • Can this evolve incrementally, or does the next requirement force a rewrite?
  • Is there a rollout and rollback plan?

1. Introduction & Purpose

This Solution Architecture Document (SAD) defines the target architecture for ClaimSight AI, an AI-augmented claims intake, triage, and fraud-signal platform for Meridian Mutual Insurance. It translates the already-approved business and compliance requirements into a structure: the services, data stores, integration points, and quality-attribute targets required to deliver the initiative. This document is the primary reference for engineering teams building the platform and for the Architecture Review Board approving it. It should be read alongside the C4 Diagrams, Architecture Decision Records, and NFR Specification, which this document references rather than duplicates.

2. Business Context & Drivers

Meridian Mutual Insurance writes personal auto and homeowners coverage for approximately 1.2 million policyholders across 14 states. Claims intake volume has grown 40% over three years (see Figure C-1 in the Case Study Brief) without a corresponding increase in adjuster headcount, producing three compounding problems: (1) average FNOL-to-adjuster-assignment time of 14 hours, well outside the 2-hour target Meridian's product organization has set for competitive parity; (2) inconsistent manual severity triage across regional call centers, driving uneven adjuster workload and inconsistent customer experience; (3) an SIU fraud-referral rate below industry benchmark, indicating likely under-detection rather than genuinely low fraud incidence. A fourth, sharper problem is catastrophe surge: regional hurricane events spike FNOL volume 5–8x within 72 hours, and the current manual-triage process has no mechanism to absorb that burst without emergency overtime staffing and multi-day backlogs.

ClaimSight AI is scoped to address all four directly: automated structured extraction from FNOL submissions (including handwritten police reports and phone-camera damage photos), automated severity triage and routing, an explainable fraud-signal score for SIU review, and an architecture that absorbs 8x burst load without manual intervention.

4. Solution Overview

ClaimSight AI is an event-driven microservices platform (justified in ADR-001) sitting alongside, not inside, Meridian's existing systems. FNOL submissions enter through a web/mobile intake app and call-center tooling, pass through an API gateway, and are published as events on a managed event bus. A Document Extraction Service performs OCR and LLM-based structured extraction on attachments; a Triage & Routing Engine applies business rules plus a severity classifier; a Fraud Scoring Service produces a fraud-signal score with reason codes; and a Case Assignment service routes the claim to an adjuster queue or SIU. Legacy integration runs through dedicated adapters that isolate PolicyBase's batch nature and ClaimsCore's existing API from the rest of the platform (ADR-005). Full structural detail is in the C4 Container and Component diagrams; the corresponding request flow is in the Sequence Diagram.

7. Deployment View

Each container is deployed as an independently scalable service in managed Kubernetes, fronted by the API gateway and organized into three environments (dev, staging, production) with identical compose/manifest definitions to guarantee dev/prod parity. The event bus, PostgreSQL cluster, and object storage are managed services outside the Kubernetes cluster to reduce operational surface area. Auto-scaling is configured per service against queue depth (for consumers of the event bus) and request latency (for the API gateway and intake path), which is what allows catastrophe-surge capacity to be elastic rather than permanently provisioned. Multi-AZ deployment within a single cloud region satisfies the RPO ≤ 5 min / RTO ≤ 1 hour targets in the NFR Specification; multi-region is explicitly out of scope for Phase 1 (see Roadmap).

8. Security & Compliance

  • TLS 1.2+ in transit; AES-256 at rest for all data stores and object storage.
  • Field-level tokenization for SSN, driver's license number, and other direct identifiers before they reach the data lake or any LLM extraction call.
  • Role-based access control with a dedicated SIU visibility scope — fraud-flag detail is not visible to standard adjuster roles.
  • Every AI-influenced routing or fraud-flag decision persists a reason-code record for the full regulatory retention window, to satisfy DOI adverse-action inquiries under the NAIC Model Bulletin.
  • All external calls (document intelligence API, fraud consortium database, payment system) run through a circuit breaker with logged request/response metadata (secrets redacted) — see Failure-First requirements in the NFR Specification.

10. Integration Approach

PolicyBase (legacy mainframe PAS) exposes only a nightly batch SFTP file — no real-time API exists today, and replacing it is out of scope (see ADR-005). ClaimSight AI integrates via a dedicated PAS Adapter that maintains a locally-cached, near-real-time read replica of policy and coverage data, refreshed nightly by the batch file and reconciled continuously against a change-data-capture feed introduced at the mainframe's existing extract layer. This isolates PolicyBase's batch nature behind an anti-corruption layer so the rest of the platform can treat policy lookups as a normal synchronous call. ClaimsCore (CMS of record) already exposes a stable API; the CMS Adapter writes claim records and status updates through it, keeping ClaimsCore as the system of record it already is rather than duplicating ownership of claim state.

ADR-001 — Adopt Event-Driven Microservices

Accepted
Context

Baseline FNOL volume is ~8,000/day but catastrophe events spike volume to ~64,000/day (8x) within 72 hours. Document extraction latency is variable (200ms–5min depending on document complexity), but intake acceptance must stay under 800ms p95 regardless. A full audit trail of every claim decision is a regulatory requirement, not a nice-to-have.

Decision

Adopt an event-driven microservices architecture: intake, extraction, triage, and fraud scoring are independent services communicating primarily through a managed event bus, with a small number of synchronous calls where a same-flow response is required.

Alternatives Considered
  • Modular monolith — rejected: cannot give document extraction (slow, spiky) independent scaling from intake (must stay fast), and a single deployable makes 8x surge capacity mean 8x permanent compute cost.
  • Synchronous microservices (REST/gRPC only, no event bus) — rejected: surge bursts of 4,000 req/hour would require synchronous backpressure handling and aggressive pre-provisioning; no natural buffering, and no audit-trail-by-construction.
Consequences
  • Positive: surge absorption via event-bus buffering; independent scaling per service; audit trail is a byproduct of the event log.
  • Negative: eventual consistency between services; requires investment in schema governance, dead-letter queues, and event replay tooling that a monolith would not need.

ADR-002 — Use Managed Kafka-Compatible Event Streaming

Accepted
Context

ADR-001 requires an event backbone capable of absorbing an 8x, multi-day burst without manual operator intervention, and Meridian's platform team does not currently run stateful streaming infrastructure in-house.

Decision

Use a managed, Kafka-API-compatible streaming service rather than operating the backbone ourselves.

Alternatives Considered
  • Self-hosted Apache Kafka — rejected as the default: lowest vendor lock-in and lowest run-rate cost at steady state, but highest operational burden precisely during the catastrophe events when the team can least afford to be firefighting infrastructure. See the full scored comparison in the Trade-off Matrix.
  • Cloud-native queue only (no log-based streaming) — rejected: loses replay-for-audit and multi-consumer fan-out that the fraud-model retraining loop depends on.
Consequences
  • Positive: elastic surge capacity without a standing ops burden; managed SLAs; faster Phase 1 delivery.
  • Negative: ongoing vendor cost scales with throughput; moderate lock-in risk, mitigated by staying on the Kafka wire protocol (see Trade-off Matrix ADR note).

ADR-003 — Buy Commercial Document Intelligence (Extraction)

Accepted
Context

~35% of FNOL submissions include handwritten police reports or phone-camera damage photos rather than clean structured forms. Extraction accuracy on this non-standard content is a gating requirement, not just a scored criterion — see the full analysis in Deliverable 5.

Decision

License a managed, multimodal LLM-based document-intelligence API for extraction rather than building an in-house model.

Alternatives Considered
  • Build in-house OCR + custom model (e.g., fine-tuned OSS layout model) — rejected: scored lowest in the trade-off matrix on accuracy and integration effort, and Meridian has no existing MLOps capacity to own model retraining on a 9-month timeline.
  • Managed structured-forms-only OCR — rejected despite the highest blended trade-off score, because it fails the gating handwriting/photo accuracy threshold required for a meaningful share of real submissions.
Consequences
  • Positive: day-1 handling of handwritten and photographic documents; no in-house ML team required to launch Phase 1.
  • Negative: per-document vendor cost at scale; data leaves Meridian's infrastructure for processing (mitigated by field-level PII tokenization before any external call — see NFR Specification, Security).

ADR-004 — Polyglot Persistence Over a Single Relational Store

Accepted
Context

Claims data has genuinely different shapes and access patterns: structured transactional claim state, large binary documents, ML feature vectors needed at sub-second latency for scoring, and historical data used for BI and model retraining.

Decision

Use PostgreSQL for transactional claims data, dedicated object storage for raw documents, a managed feature store for model-serving features, and a cloud data warehouse/lake for analytics — rather than one relational database for everything.

Alternatives Considered
  • Single PostgreSQL instance for all data classes, including documents as BLOBs and features as tables — rejected: document BLOBs would bloat the transactional database and degrade backup/restore times; feature-store access patterns (low-latency point lookups at scoring time) don't fit a general-purpose OLTP schema well.
Consequences
  • Positive: each store is right-sized for its access pattern; feature store keeps training/serving feature parity, avoiding a common source of silent model-quality drift.
  • Negative: four operational surfaces instead of one; requires explicit data-ownership documentation so it's always clear which store is authoritative for a given fact (addressed in the SAD, Section 9).

ADR-005 — Integrate PolicyBase via CDC Adapter, Not Direct API or Replacement

Accepted
Context

PolicyBase (legacy mainframe PAS) exposes only a nightly batch SFTP file. Replacing it is explicitly out of budget and timeline scope. Triage decisions need near-real-time policy and coverage data, not data that's up to 24 hours stale.

Decision

Build a PAS Adapter implementing an anti-corruption layer: a locally-cached policy read model refreshed nightly by the existing batch file, kept current intra-day via a change-data-capture feed added at the mainframe's existing extract layer, and continuously reconciled against the nightly file as the source of truth.

Alternatives Considered
  • Direct synchronous mainframe API calls — rejected: no such API exists today, and building one is a multi-quarter mainframe-side project outside this initiative's control and budget.
  • Full PAS replacement or migration — rejected outright by the approved constraints; also disproportionate risk for a claims-triage initiative to take on a core policy-system rewrite as a dependency.
Consequences
  • Positive: ClaimSight AI gets near-real-time policy context without touching PolicyBase; the anti-corruption layer means PolicyBase's COBOL data quirks never leak into the rest of the platform's domain model.
  • Negative: reconciliation mismatches are possible between the CDC feed and the nightly batch file (tracked as Risk R-11 in the Risk Register); adds one more moving part to operate and monitor.

ADR-006 — Use a Managed LLM API for Extraction & Summarization (Revisit at Scale)

Accepted — scheduled for review
Context

Document extraction (ADR-003) and adjuster-facing claim summarization both depend on a foundation-model capability. Meridian has no existing MLOps function to host and operate a foundation model in-house today.

Decision

Use a managed LLM API (SOC 2-compliant vendor) for Phase 1 and Phase 2, with an explicit Phase 3 trigger to re-evaluate self-hosting an open-source model once volume and in-house MLOps maturity justify the fixed cost of ownership.

Alternatives Considered
  • Self-hosted open-source LLM from day one — rejected for Phase 1: would require building GPU infrastructure and an MLOps function inside the 9-month pilot window, directly threatening the launch date for a capability (model hosting) that isn't Meridian's competitive differentiator (see Build vs Buy in Foundations).
Consequences
  • Positive: fastest path to a compliant Phase 1 launch; vendor carries model-quality and uptime SLAs.
  • Negative: per-call cost scales with volume and could exceed self-hosted TCO once catastrophe-scale volume is sustained; vendor lock-in risk (tracked as Risk R-04). This decision has a named revisit trigger rather than being permanent by default.
